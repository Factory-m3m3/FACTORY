The ApexPad Meme Coin Factory launches a token in a single transaction, then gives up every power over it inside that same transaction. This page shows where that is written, and what it does not cover.

01What this page proves

And, just as importantly, what it does not.

A memecoin launcher asks for enormous trust over an operation that lasts a few seconds: you sign a transaction, and a contract you did not write takes control of your token's entire supply. The only acceptable answer to that demand is to make the trust unnecessary.

So this page asks you to believe none of my claims. Each one points either to a line of source code publicly verified on the explorer, or to a value you can read yourself on-chain, in a few clicks, with no terminal and no wallet connection.

The rule we hold ourselves to

Nothing is asserted here that a third party cannot verify. Where a limitation exists — and several do — it is written as such, not left out. Section 11 is devoted entirely to them.

Said up front

These contracts are not audited. "Verified" on an explorer means the published source code matches the deployed bytecode exactly. It does not mean an independent firm has reviewed it. The two words are used separately throughout this page, and never one for the other.

02The factory has no owner

Not "renounced": never had one.

Most launchers keep an admin able to change fees, pause things, redirect funds. The MemeCoinFactory contract has none of that. Its contract declaration is bare:

contract MemeCoinFactory {

No Ownable inheritance, no onlyOwner modifier anywhere in the file, no admin function at all. There is therefore no address in the world with any special power over this factory — not mine, not any other.

What is set is set permanently, at deployment, as immutable — that is, burned into the bytecode, unreadable other than through the code itself and impossible to rewrite:

Frozen valueWhat it is
UNISWAP_V3_FACTORYThe chain's official Uniswap v3 factory
POSITION_MANAGERThe official Uniswap v3 position manager
WETHThe chain's official WETH
SWAP_ROUTERThe official Uniswap v3 swap router
PLATFORMThe address receiving the platform's 50% — see section 05

You can see this without reading me: open the factory address on your chain's explorer (section 08), Read Contract tab. There is no owner() function. Write Contract tab: there is exactly one function, launchToken, and anyone can call it.

03One transaction, ten steps

Everything succeeds together, or nothing exists.

launchToken() runs the whole sequence in a single transaction. That is the central point: there is no gap between steps during which anyone — me included — could step in.

  1. Token deployment — 100% of the supply is minted at once, to the factory.
  2. Pool creation and initialization — Uniswap v3, token/WETH, 1% tier.
  3. Reading the real tick computed by the pool itself — no home-made reimplementation of Uniswap's maths.
  4. FeeSplitter deployment with creator = msg.sender, meaning you.
  5. LiquidityCustodian deployment, with its fee recipient already pointing at that FeeSplitter.
  6. Position mint on Uniswap v3, 100% single-sided, then an explicit transfer of the NFT to the custodian.
  7. Dust sweep of any token left on the factory, into the FeeSplitter — not to an arbitrary address.
  8. Warm-up: a real buy then a real sell, in that same transaction. If they fail, the whole transaction reverts.
  9. Renouncing ownership of the token.
  10. Renouncing ownership of the custodian.
What the warm-up guarantees

Step 8 buys then sells back a small amount of the token before any renouncing happens. If the swap fails for any reason — malformed pool, invalid tick range, incompatible router — the entire transaction reverts and the token never existed.

Consequence: no stillborn token can come out of this factory. A token that exists is a token with on-chain proof that it was bought and sold at least once — in the very block of its creation, before anyone lost their powers over it. The WETH recovered on the sell, minus both swap fees, is sent back to the creator.

04Renouncing happens in the transaction

Not an intention announced for "later".

"Ownership renounced" is the easiest sentence to write in a whitepaper, and the easiest never to honour. Here it is not written in a whitepaper: it is written in the contract, two lines before the end of the launch function.

// 10. Renounce ownership of the token
token.renounceOwnership();

// 10bis. Renounce ownership of the custodian too
custodian.renounceOwnership();

Both calls sit inside the body of launchToken(). They are not in a script to run later, nor in a function the creator would have to remember to call.

The consequence, verifiable

For every token out of this factory, owner() returns 0x0000...0000 from its very first block of existence. There has never been a single block during which a human address controlled a token launched here.

Why the factory owns the custodian, and not you

At step 5, the custodian is created with the factory as owner — not the creator. That is not power retention; it is the opposite.

The only power a custodian's owner has is to change the fee recipient. If the creator owned it, they could at any moment redirect 100% of the fees to themselves and simply delete the platform's share. And if the platform kept ownership, it could do the reverse at your expense.

So the factory holds it for a few instructions, then gives it up at step 10bis, in the same transaction. Result: the fee recipient stays pointed at the FeeSplitter forever, and nobody — not you, not me, not the factory — can call setFeeRecipient() any more. The 50/50 rule becomes a property of the contract, not a commitment.

05The 50%, line by line

The easiest claim to betray on a launch platform.

Every launch deploys its own FeeSplitter contract, with two addresses frozen at construction and never modifiable afterwards:

FieldValueFrozen?
creatorThe address that signed launchToken() — youimmutable
platform0x3a7a125D23689d8F7f218B28f19305d33b6c9B18immutable

The split itself is three lines, and there is no other way out for funds in this contract:

uint256 creatorAmount  = balance / 2;
uint256 platformAmount = balance - creatorAmount;

transfer(creator,  creatorAmount);
transfer(platform, platformAmount);
The detail missing everywhere else

On an odd balance, balance / 2 rounds down, so the platform receives 1 wei more than the creator. A wei is 0.000000000000000001 ETH. It is written here because "50/50" is false to the wei, and a proof page that rounds its own numbers is worthless.

Nobody can withhold your share

release() is permissionless: anyone can trigger it, including you, including a stranger. The destinations, meanwhile, are frozen. So you depend on no action of mine to receive your half — and I cannot hold it back.

Same upstream: collect() on the custodian, which pulls the fees accumulated in the Uniswap pool into the FeeSplitter, is likewise callable by anyone.

An example you can open right now

FeeSplitter 0x287c63A1...529e473f on Ethereum, verified "Exact Match", shows its constructor arguments in plain text at the bottom of the Contract tab:

Arg [0] creator_  : 0x9969927832109E604A33749cA78C605Dea011bFB
Arg [1] platform_ : 0x3a7a125D23689d8F7f218B28f19305d33b6c9B18

An amusing and verifiable detail: that same FeeSplitter address exists identically on Ethereum, Arbitrum, Base and Optimism. It is not a coincidence — the address of a contract created by another contract depends only on the creating address and its transaction counter. Same factory, same launch rank, hence the same address. It is a mechanical consequence of the EVM, checkable on all four explorers.

Three limitations, said plainly

The fees in question are Uniswap v3 swap fees (1% per trade). With no volume there are no fees — 50% of zero is still zero. This factory does not create volume.

creator is frozen. If you lose the key to the address that signed the launch, your share becomes unreachable, permanently, for everyone.

Fees sit in the pool until collect() is called, then in the FeeSplitter until release() is called. Neither fires on its own.

06Liquidity cannot come back out

Verifiable by absence of code, not by commitment.

The Uniswap v3 position NFT is held by the launch's LiquidityCustodian. That contract can do three things, and three only: receive the position once, collect accumulated fees, and change the fee recipient for as long as it has an owner — which, as we saw, ends inside the launch transaction. It also exposes one public read, currentLiquidity(), which changes nothing.

What matters here is not what it does, but what it does not contain:

FunctionPresent?Consequence
decreaseLiquidity()AbsentLiquidity cannot be reduced
burn()AbsentThe position cannot be destroyed
Outgoing NFT transferAbsentThe position cannot leave the contract
selfdestructAbsentThe contract cannot disappear
Proxy / upgradeAbsentThe code cannot be replaced

A guarantee by absence is stronger than a guarantee by promise: it depends on no future behaviour. The public currentLiquidity() function lets anyone read the position's liquidity at any time and confirm it never goes down.

What "locked liquidity" does NOT mean here

The position is 100% single-sided: at launch, only the token's entire supply is deposited into the pool. No ETH is deposited by the creator or by the platform. The ETH sitting in the pool is the buyers' ETH, arriving purchase by purchase.

Concretely: there is no treasury guaranteeing a price floor, and some scanners will display "LP locked: 0%" because they look for LP tokens locked with a third-party service, which is not the mechanism used here.

On the other hand — and this is the point that matters — that accumulated ETH cannot be taken out by anyone except the sellers themselves, by selling back into the market. Neither the creator nor I can pull it from the pool: the only function that would allow it, decreaseLiquidity(), does not exist in the contract holding the position.

07The token: no mint, no tax

A deliberately boring ERC-20.

The MemeToken contract deployed at each launch is a plain ERC-20, with no extra layer. The total supply is minted once, in the constructor, and stored as immutable.

Usual mechanismPresent?
Mint function after deploymentNone
Buy or sell taxNone
Address blacklist / whitelistNone
Transfer pauseNone
Maximum per walletNone
Cooldown between transactionsNone

None of these are disabled by configuration: the transfer logic is simply not overridden. What applies is the standard ERC-20 transfer, without a single condition tied to the pool, the clock or the block number.

Apart from the ownership functions themselves, the only owner-restricted function this token ever had is setPool(), which records the pool address once and for all, purely for information — and it is called at step 6, before the renouncing. After that, nobody can call it.

A deliberate absence: no anti-sniping

There is intentionally no anti-bot delay at launch. Two reasons, and the first is the less flattering one: a fixed delay protects nobody. Once the countdown ends, a bot is still faster than a human — the delay moves the race, it does not cancel it.

The second: these temporary transfer-blocking mechanisms trigger "honeypot" false positives on several scanners, which damages the contract's readability for a careful buyer. We preferred a contract anyone can read in full over a protection that is not one.

09What a third party says

And, as everywhere else on this page, what it does not say.

Blockaid is a security provider whose classifications feed the warnings several wallets display at the moment you sign a transaction. On 17 August 2026, after review, their team confirmed two things in writing:

  • the factory address 0xa33F5330...e87049C8 — the one used on Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain, Base, Optimism and Arbitrum — is classified "Benign";
  • the domain apexpad.io is whitelisted on their end.
The test you can run yourself

A copy of an email proves nothing: it takes three minutes to fabricate. The check that matters is within your reach and does not depend on my word — open the launch tool and connect your wallet. No "deceptive request" or "untrusted site" warning should appear. That is exactly what a classification like the one above produces, and it is observable live, right now.

Four caveats, because they matter

Blockaid is not an auditor and does not present itself as one. "Benign" means no known malicious pattern was detected, not that the code was reviewed line by line. Section 11 stands in full.

The technical description in that ticket is mine, not a Blockaid conclusion: it is the body of my own report, quoted back verbatim in the thread. The facts it states are accurate and verifiable — sections 02 to 07 show you where — but they were not validated one by one by Blockaid.

A classification is not permanent. It can be revised at any time, in either direction. That is why the date appears above rather than a badge.

11What remains on you

The most important section on this page.

Everything above is about one thing: what the code prevents. Here, in the same spirit, is what it does not.

These contracts are not audited

They are verified on seven explorers, which guarantees that the code you read is the code that runs. No independent firm has reviewed them. The source file itself carries a NOT AUDITED notice at the top, and the launch interface displays it. A bug remains possible, including one that would falsify one of the guarantees above. You have my good faith and the code — not a third party's signature.

A memecoin can go to zero

None of the guarantees on this page concern price. Liquidity that cannot be withdrawn is not liquidity that supports a price. A token launched here can lose all of its value, and most do.

The factory does not vet projects

The name, ticker, supply and starting market cap are chosen freely by whoever launches. Nothing is checked: not the originality of the name, not the existence of a team, not the slightest intention. A token launched by this factory is not endorsed by ApexPad. The mechanism is safe; what the creator does with it afterwards is not up to us.

The creator keeps their token — and can sell it

The step-8 warm-up returns their WETH, and nothing stops them from buying their own token on the market like anyone else. What the contract guarantees is that they receive no free allocation at creation: the entire supply goes into the pool, bar rounding dust, which is itself swept into the FeeSplitter at step 7 and therefore split 50/50 like any other fee. Whatever they buy afterwards, they buy at market price, visible on-chain to everyone.

Never send ETH directly to the factory

The factory accepts native ETH, because it must be able to receive some during the warm-up. It has no withdrawal function. ETH sent straight to its address, outside a call to launchToken(), would stay stuck there forever — including for me. Always go through the launch interface.

The 50% depends on a key only you hold

Already said in section 05, repeated here because it is irreversible: the creator's share is locked to the address that signed the launch. Lost, it is lost for everyone.

12Check it yourself, in 6 clicks

No terminal, nothing to install, no wallet connection.

Take any token from the catalogue and its proof sheet, which gives you its addresses. Then, on its chain's explorer:

  1. The factory — open its address (section 08), Contract tab. You should see the green verification check and the full source code. Scroll down: the constructor arguments show _platform = 0x3a7a125D...3b6c9B18.
  2. Still the factory, Read Contract tab. Go through the list: there is no owner(). Write Contract tab: there is only launchToken.
  3. The token — open its address, Read Contract tab, click owner(). Expected answer: 0x0000000000000000000000000000000000000000.
  4. The custodian — same operation. owner() returns 0x0000...0000, and feeRecipient() returns the FeeSplitter address of that same launch. Nobody can change it any more.
  5. The FeeSplitter — Read Contract, then creator() and platform(). The first is the address that launched the token, the second is the platform address, identical across all launches.
  6. The Uniswap position — look up the sheet's position number on the chain's NonfungiblePositionManager: its holder is the custodian. Then, on the custodian, currentLiquidity(): come back and read it whenever you like, it will never go down.
If a single one of these six points does not hold

Then this page is lying, and you have public proof of where. Say so publicly. That is exactly the point of writing proofs rather than promises: they can be refuted.
